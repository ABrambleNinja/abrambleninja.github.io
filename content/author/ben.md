hey! welcome to my website. i hope to post interesting things here sometimes!

i'm a software engineer who likes getting sucked into interesting technical problems.
when i'm not programming, i like to climb, make things, and struggle for a better future for all living beings on this planet.

<!-- more -->

my software engineering-adjacent interests include (but are by no means limited to) strongly typed languages, algorithms/data structures, computational complexity, and performant code.
i love the theory side of things and enjoy reading papers that are basically math research, but i'm also very interested in where the rubber meets the road and understanding how code actually runs on the physical hardware.
in undergrad, though i majored in computer science, i took a substantial number of computer engineering courses, up through vlsi circuit design (for the vlsi final project, my team designed an 8-bit multicycle processor from scratch, from the instruction set to the verilog to the silicon layout, and we even got it fabricated).

my master's degree was a jog in the other direction, where i focused almost exclusively on cs theory, and in particular, randomized algorithms.
for my thesis, i discovered a las vegas-style randomized algorithm that solves the majority vote problem using fewer comparisons than the previous state of the art.

after finishing my degrees, i worked at intel in the programmable systems group (which has since been spun off as [a separate company](https://altera.com/)).
there, i worked on the quartus fpga compiler.
my team was responsible for the 'planning' phase of the compilation, during which there are preliminary decisions made about how to arrange a design onto the chip, e.g. clock routing and periphery devices.
some of the most interesting things that i did there include:

1. proved the NP-completeness of a routing/allocation problem that arose in network-on-chip design, then described an algorithm to transform an instance of the problem into an integer linear program that could be solved by a specialized solver
2. improved logic for deciding when to move registers around within the routing fabric in order to meet timing guarantees, allowing for timing to be met on cases when not previously possible
3. wrote a parser for a domain specific language that specifies connectivity between multiple fpga dies, in order to prototype and iterate on designs for future multi-die fpgas

i also taught [cmpt 307](https://www.sfu.ca/students/calendar/courses/cmpt/307.html) at simon fraser university, which was a fun one-off experience (but probably not something i'd want to do regularly).
it definitely gave me a new perspective on being an instructor (as well as even more respect for some of the professors i've had), and also gave me a lot of good practice with explaining technical concepts.
i've known for a long time that teaching and explaining technical concepts is something that i enjoy doing, so even if i don't plan to do that as the primary way that i sell my labor-power, i want to practice that skill by writing things here.

since leaving intel, i have been taking some time off of work to un-burn-out and fix up my mental health.
during this time, i re-discovered my love of programming, learned rust, read a lot, and worked on personal projects, some of which will be detailed on this very website.
